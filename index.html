    How the estimate works

    Each answer maps to typical published sound ratings for that kind of construction. STC (Sound Transmission Class) rates how well a wall or floor blocks airborne sound like voices and TV. IIC (Impact Insulation Class) rates how well a floor blocks footsteps. Higher is better for both.

    The numbers shown are field values: what a wall achieves in a finished building, usually 3 to 8 points below its lab rating because sound also leaks through outlets, ducts, gaps and the structure itself. US building codes require 50 in the lab, or 45 when tested in the field, between dwellings. Many buildings older than the 1980s were never built to that.

    The quiet score combines the wall and floor estimates, weighted toward the walls if a bedroom shares one, and subtracts points for noisy neighbors like elevators and trash chutes. A wall test of the neighbor wall replaces the wall estimate.

    Real buildings vary. Two units in the same building can differ by 5 points or more depending on workmanship. Treat the result as a range, not a verdict.

    Measure a wall

    Play steady noise on one side of a wall, then measure how much quieter it is on the other side. The phone doesn't need to be calibrated, because only the difference counts.

    Test noise

    Plays steady pink noise. A Bluetooth speaker works best; a phone speaker is only loud enough to test doors and weak walls. You can also open this page on a second phone or laptop and play the noise there. Point the speaker at the wall from about 1 m away, and keep the volume the same for steps 2 and 3.         How accurate is this?

        Estimate. Built from typical published ratings. Real buildings vary by 5 points or more because of workmanship, back-to-back outlets, shared ducts and sound traveling through the structure.

        Wall test. Uses the same method as professional field tests (ASTM E336, ISO 16283): a level difference between two rooms, corrected for background noise and room echo, rated against the ISO 717 reference curve in octave bands. A phone mic, few positions and a small speaker make it rougher, about ±3 to 5 points. Bass below 125 Hz isn't measured, and a phone speaker can't reach the lowest bands at all.

        Room noise. Phones aren't calibrated, so absolute levels may be off by 5 dB or more until you calibrate. Rooms quieter than about 30 dB(A) are near the microphone's own noise floor, so the real level may be lower than shown.
